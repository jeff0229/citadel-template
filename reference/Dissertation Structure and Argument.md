# Dissertation Structure and Argument

*Reusable craft reference. Derived from a UK taught-MSc dissertation handbook (school-wide rule book on structure, argument, referencing). Treat the structural principles as portable; verify every hard number (word count, deadline, pass thresholds, section numbering) against your own programme handbook.*

## What it informs

All chapters. Most directly: overall argument structure, the Introduction, where to start writing, how to handle the Literature Review, the Conclusion, referencing, and quotation conventions.

## Structural rules and framing moves

- **Six argument structures** (one is usually primary for the whole document). Categorical / Evaluative / Chronological / Comparative / Sequential / Causal. Pick the one that matches your overarching claim. Sub-sections may run on a different structure from the whole.
- **Three argument levels.** Empirical ("what the world is like") / Conceptual ("what it all means") / Methodological ("how we know"). Identify which level your contribution sits at. Many dissertations combine two, most often Conceptual and Methodological.
- **Generic chapter structure** (one handbook gives a 15,000-word worked example; programmes vary, so confirm your own figure). Introduction (~2,500 words) / 3 to 4 substantive chapters (~2,500 to 3,000 words each) / Conclusion (~1,000 to 2,000 words). Programme-specific word counts override this example.
- **The Introduction** spells out the focus, the objectives or research questions, why these are interesting, and locates them in the field. It includes an outline of the subsequent chapters. Often best written last.
- **Methodology** is usually the best place to start the entire writing process, if the dissertation includes one.
- **Literature Review handling.** Not always required as a separate chapter. Options are: fold it into the Introduction, give it a dedicated background chapter, or weave it through. Discuss with your supervisor.
- **Each substantive chapter** must have a clear opening and closing that, between them, signal (a) how the chapter fits into the rest of the dissertation, (b) the chapter's internal structure, and (c) the main point the reader should take from it.
- **Signpost at every level.** Put core points at the start of paragraphs, sections, and chapters. Link explicitly between them.
- **Diagnostic for being stuck.** Returning to the structure of the overall argument usually resolves the "I do not know what to write next" feeling. Reach for the argument-structure choice rather than the keyboard.
- **Quotations.** Use sparingly, only when the wording is striking. Over 40 words becomes a block quote, indented and single-spaced.
- **Sources.** Be cautious of internet sources without quality control. News media is a primary source for public interventions, polls, and editorials, not a substitute for academic research.
- **Page numbers** are required in every citation, even when not directly quoting, unless referring to the gist or to an argument running throughout a work.

## Exemplar passages worth modelling

> "Almost every time when students 'feel lost' and 'don't know what to write next,' it is because they have not yet worked out what the structure of their overall argument is."

The load-bearing diagnostic. When stuck, settle the argument structure first.

> "The introduction spells out the focus of the study and its objectives or research questions, explaining why these were interesting to the author and 'locating' them in the field. It should also include an outline of the subsequent chapters."

The Introduction's job description in one sentence: focus plus research questions plus interest plus field-location plus chapter outline.

> "Each substantive chapter should have a clear beginning and end which between them signpost clearly (a) how this chapter fits into the rest of the dissertation, (b) the structure of the chapter, (c) the main points which the reader should take from it."

The (a)/(b)/(c) test for any chapter opener and closer.

## Caveats

- The "15,000-word" figure is a generic example. Programme-specific word counts override it. Verify your own figure separately.
- "Apply relevant theoretical insights and reflect critically on their adequacy" is broad licence to push back on the theory you cite where your data warrants, not just to summarise it.
- Many programmes give the supervisor only one draft chapter to read, not the whole dissertation. Plan for that. Submit the chapter most in need of feedback early.
