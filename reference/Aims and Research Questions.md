# Aims and Research Questions

*Reusable craft reference. Derived from a UK university lecture on designing and refining research questions for qualitative MSc research (Plowman). Topic-neutral and directly templatable. The verbatim frameworks (SMART, aim-verb list, RQ stems) are reproduced for reuse.*

## What it informs

The Introduction (aim and RQ formulation). The Methodology (RQ-method alignment). The Conclusion (outcomes scaled to RQs).

## Structural rules and framing moves

- **Aim first, then RQs.** The aim is one sentence, one overall aim. The research questions decompose it.
- **Maximum 3 to 4 RQs.** Avoid more.
- **Each RQ must** be answerable, have a potentially interesting answer, be on the right scale (not too big or small, not yes/no, not too open or closed), be one question only, guide method choice, and have a clear relationship to the aim.
- **RQs decompose the aim.** Each typically maps to one key word or theme of the aim, or to a combination of them.
- **Iterative refinement is mandatory.** Expect to cycle through refining aims and RQs several times. Allow plenty of time; this part cannot be rushed.
- **All elements must interlink.** Rationale, aims, RQs, methods, and outcomes must align.
- **Design via an RQs grid.** Columns for Research Question, Data Collection, Data Analysis and Interpretation, Outcomes. One data source can answer more than one RQ; one RQ may need more than one source.
- **Framing-frame metaphor.** Visualise concentric circles. What is the centre? What is the next ring (linked but not the main focus)? What is excluded? Change the frame and you change what you see.
- **MSc-scale outcomes are modest.** "A description or account of...", "a framework for...", "guidelines for...". Avoid claiming changes in policy, practice, or curriculum, or "proof that...".
- **Avoid grounded theory at MSc scale** (hard to do well, takes more time than you have). Avoid vague invocations of "thematic analysis"; specify the variant. The analysis stage is usually the weakest area, so plan it concretely.

## SMART framework (verbatim)

> "Specific — The aim should specify what you want to achieve succinctly. It should be focused, concrete and well-defined.
> Measurable — How will you know if you've met the aim? This doesn't need to be a quantitative measure, but you will need to provide evidence of how you met the overall aim and that your conclusions are credible. You can't do this if the aim is expressed very vaguely.
> Achievable — Is the aim achievable, feasible and actionable?
> Realistic — Can you realistically achieve the aim with the person power and resources (time, skills or money) that you have?
> Tractable — Is the framing of the project manageable and controllable?"

## Aim-verb list (verbatim)

> "The aim of this study is...
> to account for...
> to investigate...
> to understand...
> to describe...
> to explore...
> to identify the factors that...
> to consider the benefits and challenges of...
> [more difficult for small-scale research:]
> to evaluate...
> to compare..."

Direct verb-pickers for the aim sentence. "Evaluate" and "compare" are flagged as harder at MSc scale. If your project is inherently comparative or evaluative, compensate by leading with a primary verb such as "understand" or "identify the factors that", and by being explicit about the comparison's tractability.

## RQ-stem templates (verbatim)

> "How do [actors]... ?
> What opportunities..., resources..., key differences..., changes... ?
> What are the advantages and disadvantages...?
> What are the arrangements for...?
> What is the provision for...?
> To what extent...?
> Be careful with questions like How effective...?"

"How do [actor]...?" maps cleanly onto most qualitative interview or ethnographic studies. "To what extent...?" fits scaled claims about a factor's role. Avoid "How effective...?".

## Exemplar three-RQ structure (verbatim, generic worked example)

> "RQ1: what is the [object/content of the thing studied]?
> RQ2: how do [actors] interact with [the thing] in ways that [produce the outcome of interest]?
> RQ3: what are [participants'] perspectives on [the outcome]?"

A clean object/interaction/perspective triad: RQ1 describes the object, RQ2 examines the interaction that produces the outcome, RQ3 captures participants' own accounts. A reusable shape for many qualitative designs.

## Caveats

- Run your draft RQs against the checklist: total count no more than 4; each is one question (no hidden conjunctions); none starts with "how effective" or "evaluate"; each maps clearly to one key word of a single overall aim.
- This source is silent on theoretical-framing depth. See [[Argumentation and Critical Thinking]].
- The advice is pitched at a roughly 12,000 to 15,000-word MSc dissertation. The SMART and scale guidance transfers down to shorter dissertations, but confirm your own word count.
