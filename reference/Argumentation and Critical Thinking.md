# Argumentation and Critical Thinking

*Reusable craft reference. Derived from Andrews (2007), a guide to argumentation and critical thinking in the postgraduate dissertation (UK Education tradition). Topic-neutral. Supplies the argumentative-versus-expository distinction that structural handbooks imply but rarely spell out.*

## What it informs

Argument structure across all chapters. Most directly the Introduction, Literature Review, Discussion, and Conclusion. Critical-thinking posture throughout.

## Structural rules and framing moves

- **Six principles for argumentative postgraduate writing.**
  1. Single authorial voice.
  2. Measured balance between the personal "I" and the impersonal voice.
  3. Vertical or paradigmatic structure (classification, hierarchy, distinctions).
  4. Horizontal or quasi-logical momentum (each idea connects to the next).
  5. Connections made explicit, not implied.
  6. Academic discourse features (diction, tone, detached but not disinterested energy, evidence weighed by disciplinary norms).
- **A seventh principle.** Every chapter must show critical thought ("thinking with edge"), not exposition.
- **The (a) to (f) test for whether a dissertation is argumentative.** The dissertation only counts as argumentative once it has (a) worked out a theoretical position, (b) reviewed the literature, (c) designed the empirical study, (d) gathered the evidence, (e) arrayed the evidence into categories, and (f) found its own position relative to those categories. Most students stop at step (e). Step (f) is where the argumentative work actually happens.
- **The literature review provides the backing** (in Toulmin's sense) for the whole argument. Gauge small empirical advances against the wider theoretical and research literature.
- **Use the "main path / faulty path" model during composition.** Identify the line of argument from A to B; mark off the faulty paths (positions you will not take) as reference points. Useful for the Introduction.
- **Use Toulmin (claim, grounds, warrant, backing, rebuttal) after the first draft** to test argument soundness, not as a composition scaffold.
- **Critical posture is a "vow of suspicion".** Read sceptically; do not believe by default. Detached but not disinterested.
- **Counter-argument handling.** The "Whereas X suggests..., it seems to me that..." move. Sources are used to define your position, not to pile up.
- **The "golden thread" must stay visible across chapters** via explicit rhetorical signposting (the dispositio, or arrangement).

## Exemplar passages worth modelling

> "A dissertation/thesis will not be truly argumentative until it has (a) worked out its theoretical position, (b) reviewed the literature, (c) designed an appropriate empirical study (if it is that kind of study), (d) gathered the evidence, (e) arrayed the evidence into categories and (f) found its own position in relation to those categories, arranging them in a sequence that carries the argument of the piece as a whole."

A direct checklist for whether the Discussion is doing argumentative work or merely exposition. Steps (e) and (f) are where most Discussion chapters fail.

> "Whereas X suggest that ..., it seems to me that ..."

A literal sentence template for counter-argument framing in the Introduction and Discussion. Use this grammar when positioning against the existing literature.

> "argumentative lines of enquiry are the golden thread that runs through a good thesis or dissertation. ... The visibility of the argument, made manifest in explicit rhetorical signposting, is based on the arrangement, the dispositio, of the thesis."

Justifies aggressive sub-headed signposting and recurring callbacks to the central claim across chapters.

## Caveats

- The guide writes from a UK-Anglophone tradition. Its "detached, disinterested energy" caveat acknowledges that feminist and post-structuralist work legitimately rejects that posture. Relevant if your study involves positioned researchers or participants.
- The exemplar dissertations are Education-MA scale (15,000 to 30,000 words). The principles transfer down. The argument-versus-exposition binary maps onto the Empirical / Conceptual / Methodological argument levels; steps (e) and (f) sit at the Conceptual level.
- The guide privileges a Comparative structure as the strongest student model and treats bare categorisation as insufficient for a top mark. If your design is comparative, it is well-positioned by this metric.
