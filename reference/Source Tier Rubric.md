# Source Tier Rubric

*Reusable methodology tool. A five-tier rubric for scoring the reliability of sources you read and cite, so your confidence judgements are consistent and defensible. Topic-neutral. Use it when adding any source to your reading notes or reference library.*

## Purpose

Note-taking and reference systems often attach a confidence or reliability value to each source but give no written rubric for what each level means. This rubric maps a 0.0 to 1.0 reliability scale onto five tiers, so scoring is consistent across sources and defensible if challenged.

The rubric scores **the source**, not the claim. A strong empirical study can still contain weak speculative passages; preserve that granularity with page-anchored quotes rather than a single source-level score.

---

## The five tiers

| Tier | Band | What it is | Generic examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | 0.90 to 1.0 | Primary empirical study directly relevant to your thesis. Peer-reviewed, methodology visible, datasets or field observations present. Can be cited as load-bearing. | A peer-reviewed empirical study whose finding underpins one of your core claims. |
| T2 | 0.75 to 0.89 | Strong expert analysis. Peer-reviewed theory or framework pieces, authoritative books, methodologically sound qualitative studies. Cite as supporting, not load-bearing. | A foundational theory or framework paper; a methods textbook chapter you build your design on. |
| T3 | 0.60 to 0.74 | Informed commentary, or tangential but useful. Working papers, long-form investigative journalism with citations, reference chapters whose specific framework you do not foreground. | A useful companion handbook; a credible source on an adjacent question. |
| T4 | 0.40 to 0.59 | General-interest sources, popular science, think-tank reports with unclear funding. Use for orientation only; find a T1 or T2 upstream before citing. | A trade-press explainer; an advocacy report with an unstated funder. |
| T5 | 0.0 to 0.39 | Social or anecdotal: social-media posts, blog rants. Usable as signal ("people are talking about X") but never as evidence. | A viral post; a comment thread. |

---

## Red flags (downgrade by one tier if present)

- No cited sources or visible methodology.
- The author has a direct financial incentive in the conclusion.
- Published by an organisation with a known position on the topic (advocacy publishers, agenda-driven think tanks).
- Cherry-picked timeframes or geographies that flatter the argument.
- Conflates correlation with causation without acknowledging it.
- Uses emotional language where data should do the work.

## Upgrade triggers (rare)

- Independent replication of the core finding by a different team.
- The author revises or retracts, and the updated claim is stronger.
- A longitudinal follow-up supports the original finding over time.

---

## How to score a source

1. Start with the tier that best describes the source *type*.
2. Check the red flags; downgrade if any apply.
3. Pick the numeric value within the band based on **relevance to your thesis**, not general quality:
   - High in the band (for example 0.85 in T2): directly addresses one of your core claims.
   - Low in the band (for example 0.76 in T2): a useful theoretical companion but peripheral.
4. Record the value in your notes for that source.
5. If uncertain between two tiers, pick the lower one. Better to under-confidence a source than over-cite it.

## Decay and supersession

- **Age alone does not decay a tier.** A strong source from 2004 may still be T1 today.
- What decays is *re-verification*: if you have not re-read a source in a while and it is cited in active drafting, re-read it and refresh your note.
- **Supersession is different.** A source whose core claim is later contradicted by a better study moves to T3 or lower, regardless of its original tier. Record what superseded it (see [[Contradiction Protocol]]).

---

## Applying the scale to non-source notes

If your note system also scores your own analytical pages:

- **Entity or person notes:** the score reflects how accurately you have captured that scholar's or organisation's position.
- **Concept notes:** the score reflects how stable the definition is. Well-established concepts score high; concepts you are still working out score lower.
- **Synthesis notes:** the score reflects how well-evidenced the synthesis is. High for a synthesis drawing on five or more sources with no open contradictions; lower for speculative or thinly-evidenced pieces.

---

## Caveats

- A tier is a rubric, not a guarantee. A T1 source can still be wrong. Cite it with appropriate hedging.
- The rubric scores the source, not each claim within it. Use page-anchored quotes to preserve claim-level granularity.
- The rubric is tuned for academic work. Adapt cautiously if reused for other purposes.
- This rubric is adapted from a generic source-tier idea into an academic-dissertation frame: generic reliability categories are re-cast in academic terms. A "locked" flag on stable, repeatedly-confirmed citations is a useful complement, so that mature citations do not accumulate synthetic uncertainty over time.
