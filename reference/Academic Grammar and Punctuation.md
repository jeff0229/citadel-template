# Academic Grammar and Punctuation

*Reusable craft reference. Derived from a UK university study guide on grammar and punctuation. Topic-neutral. These are the academic-writing-specific rules worth flagging; basic grammar (apostrophes, your/you're) is omitted.*

## What it informs

All chapters. Most directly the final-pass edit. The Discussion and Methodology benefit most from the rules below.

## Academic-writing-specific rules

- Treat **`data` as plural** in academic prose. "The data are...", not "the data is". A reviewer shibboleth.
- **`i.e.` and `e.g.` are not interchangeable.** `i.e.` means "in other words" and closes a set; `e.g.` means "for example" and opens a non-exhaustive set. (Corollary: an "e.g." list is examples, not a closed set.)
- Use **`try to`, not `try and`**, in formal writing.
- **`fewer`** for countable nouns, **`less`** for non-countable or hypothetical quantity.
- **`different from`** (the safe default), not "different to" or "different than".
- **No exclamation marks.** Nothing in formal writing should be exclaimed.
- **Avoid em-dashes** as comma or colon substitutes. Formal technical writing should avoid the dash as much as possible. If you must use one, use the proper em-rule glyph, not a hyphen with spaces.
- **Use italics, not underlines**, for emphasis, foreign terms, and titles. Never both.
- **`however` is an adverb, not a conjunction.** It cannot replace "but" to splice two clauses. It needs a full stop or a semicolon before it.
- **Restrictive versus non-restrictive `that` and `which`.** Non-essential clauses take "which" and a comma. Essential clauses can take either, with "that" cleaner.
- **Singular `they`** for "everyone" or "nobody" is often better than the clumsy "his or her". Reword to a plural subject where possible.
- **No full stop after headers or titles.**
- **Comma splices and run-ons** are high on the list of grammatical sins. Watch for them.
- **Parallelism in lists and bullets.** Every item must share part of speech and tense.

## Exemplar passages worth modelling

> "Grammar should be like the background music in a film. If it's good you hardly notice it. If it's bad, it jars and distracts you and does not allow you to focus on what's important."

The framing principle for the whole prose pass: invisible mechanics, not showy correctness.

> "Despite the controversy in style guides I recommend treating 'data' as a plural unless told explicitly otherwise. The plural form will upset nobody, while the singular form will raise some readers' blood pressure."

A rule worth obeying for submission, purely to minimise examiner risk.

> "'However' is an adverb, but it is often mistakenly used as an alternative to the conjunction 'but' to join phrases."

A common bite. You cannot write "the data was X, however the result was Y" with just a comma; it needs a new sentence or a semicolon.

## Optional style overlay

Some writers and supervisors prefer to **reduce colons, semicolons, and em-dashes** (the punctuation most associated with overwrought or machine-generated prose) in favour of full sentences and parentheses. Hyphens for compound modifiers stay. This goes beyond what the guide prescribes and is a matter of house style; adopt it if it suits your voice and your supervisor's preference.

## Caveats

- The guide is generic British academic English, not discipline-specific. Conventions like tense shifts between Methodology and Findings, or hedging in qualitative claims, are not covered here. Look elsewhere for those.
- Several sections are basic grammar (apostrophes, common comma uses). Skip them during dissertation drafting.
- Single versus double inverted commas: British convention is singles outside, doubles inside. Pick one convention and keep it consistent.
