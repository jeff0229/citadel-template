# Contradiction Protocol

*Reusable methodology tool. When two sources disagree, document the disagreement rather than resolve it silently. Contradictions are often where an original contribution sits. Topic-neutral. The worked examples are generic.*

## Purpose

When two sources disagree, your notes and your dissertation should *document* the disagreement, not bury it. Contradictions are load-bearing: they frequently mark where your own contribution lives. This protocol keeps contradictions surfaced.

Distinguish two kinds:

1. **Supersession** — a newer source replaces an older one; the older claim is now wrong.
2. **Live contradiction** — two sources disagree and both remain credible; the disagreement is itself a finding.

---

## Supersession

When a new source supersedes an older claim:

1. On the **old** source note, record that it is superseded by the newer source, and by what specific claim or scope.
2. On the **new** source note, record what it supersedes.
3. On any **synthesis** note that cited the old claim, add an inline flag that the claim is now contested by the newer source.
4. Do NOT rewrite the old source note to say "this was wrong." The note is a historical record of what that source argued. Mark it superseded; keep the original content intact.

---

## Live contradiction

When two credible sources disagree and neither supersedes the other:

### Step 1: Rule out trivial explanations

Before declaring a real contradiction, check for:

- **Scope mismatch** — the two sources study different populations, regions, or platforms. Both may be right in their own context.
- **Timeframe mismatch** — one measures an earlier period, the other a later one. The phenomenon may have changed.
- **Definitional mismatch** — a key term means different things in the two sources (for example, two different constructs sharing one label). Not the same thing.
- **Data-source mismatch** — both use the same data type but draw on different datasets or sampling frames.

If any of these applies, document it as a **scope distinction**, not a contradiction.

### Step 2: Identify the root of disagreement

If the contradiction is genuine, classify it:

- **Data disagreement** — different datasets produce different findings. The better data wins until both are re-audited.
- **Interpretation disagreement** — same data, different conclusions. Examine the reasoning chains; usually traceable to different theoretical priors.
- **Mechanism disagreement** — same phenomenon, different proposed causes. You are free to adjudicate based on which mechanism best fits your thesis; cite both.
- **Incentive disagreement** — one author has reason to reach a particular conclusion (for example, a stakeholder funder). Downgrade that source by one tier per the [[Source Tier Rubric]] red flags; the contradiction may resolve.

### Step 3: Write a short contradiction note

Capture the disagreement in a dedicated note:

```markdown
## Contradiction: <short title>

### The disagreement
- Source A argues <X> based on <method/data>.
- Source B argues <Y> based on <method/data>.

### Root cause
<Likely source of the disagreement: data, interpretation, mechanism, or incentive.>

### Conditions under which each is probably right
- Source A's claim probably holds when: <conditions>.
- Source B's claim probably holds when: <conditions>.

### Implication for the dissertation
<Which position you take, and why. Or: why you remain agnostic.>

### What would resolve the contradiction
<Specific evidence or replication that would settle it.>
```

### Step 4: Promote to an open question if unresolvable

If the contradiction cannot be adjudicated and the dissertation must proceed without resolving it, log it as an open question so it is visible when the literature review is drafted.

Saying "we do not know whether X or Y" in the dissertation is acceptable, provided the claim is scoped correctly and the evidence on both sides is cited. An honestly-declared open question is stronger than a silently-chosen one.

---

## When to suspect a hidden contradiction

- **All sources agree** on a claim: re-read the more contrarian sources to stress-test it. Suspicious unanimity is often citation monoculture.
- **A synthesis note has high confidence but cites only two sources:** it is probably missing the contradicting source. Run a targeted search.
- **Someone pushes back on a claim your notes support:** treat the pushback as evidence of a missing contradiction, not proof that the challenger is wrong.

---

## Worked examples (generic)

- Two sources propose different *mechanisms* for the same phenomenon (for example, a "sorting" account versus an "exposure" account). This is a mechanism disagreement: adjudicate which mechanism you adopt and why, and cite both.
- One source finds the causal arrow runs the opposite way from the dominant tradition. This is a live contradiction to engage with rather than resolve.
- Two sources describe the same kind of system in two different countries and reach different conclusions. This is usually a scope distinction, not a contradiction (handle per Step 1).
