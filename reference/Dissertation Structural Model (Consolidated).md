## Argument shape (cross-cutting)

Pick your **overarching argument structure** from the six-structure typology (Categorical, Evaluative, Chronological, Comparative, Sequential, Causal). A comparative design is often the strongest student model, but sub-sections may run on a different structure from the whole (for example, a Causal sub-argument inside a broadly Comparative chapter).

Identify the **argument level** of your contribution: Empirical ("what the world is like"), Conceptual ("what it all means"), or Methodological ("how we know"). Many dissertations combine Conceptual and Methodological: a fresh conceptual claim, evidenced by a deliberate methodological pairing. State which level your contribution sits at.

If your design is inherently comparative or evaluative, note that these are flagged as harder at MSc scale. Keep the comparison tractable: anchor it on a small number of named dimensions rather than a wholesale comparison, and defend that scope explicitly in the Introduction.

Use the (a) to (f) test as the diagnostic for whether the Discussion is doing argumentative work or merely exposition. Most dissertations stop at (e), arraying evidence into categories. Step (f), finding your own position relative to those categories, is where the argument lives.

## Chapter-by-chapter framing

### Introduction

Sources: Dissertation Structure and Argument, Aims and Research Questions, Argumentation and Critical Thinking, Literature Review Craft.

Job description in one sentence: spell out the focus, the objectives or research questions, why these are interesting, locate them in the field, and outline the subsequent chapters.

Aim formulation: one sentence, one overall aim, satisfying SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Tractable). Choose a primary verb from the aim-verb list (understand, investigate, describe, explore, identify the factors that, consider the benefits and challenges of). Avoid "evaluate" and "compare" as the primary verb at MSc scale even where the content is comparative; lead with "understand" or "identify the factors that".

Research questions: 3 to 4 maximum. Each is one question, answerable, on the right scale, guides method choice, and maps clearly to the aim. Useful stems include "How do [actors]...?", "What features of [the thing studied] shape...?", and "To what extent...?". Avoid "How effective...?".

Counter-positioning: use the "Whereas X suggests..., it seems to me that..." move to position against the existing literature. Mark off the faulty paths (positions you will not take) as reference points.

End the Introduction with an outline of the subsequent chapters. The Introduction is often best written last.

### Literature Review

Sources: Literature Review Craft, Systematic vs Narrative Review (contrast), Argumentation and Critical Thinking, Effective Reading Protocol.

Three-part structure: the introduction states scope and what is excluded; the main body synthesises; the conclusion adjudicates, identifies gaps, or segues into your own research. The conclusion does not recap.

Synthesis, not laundry list: replace serial paraphrase with integrated discussion. Use signpost connectors ("Similarly", "In contrast", "concurred with"). Two moves demonstrate critical engagement: (1) integration via similarities and differences across sources, and (2) an analytical step-back asking "so what does that all mean?", "why are there conflicting findings?", "what is missing?".

Topic-sentence paragraphs: every paragraph opens with a sentence stating what it will do.

Selection logic: document inclusion and exclusion criteria explicitly even for a narrative review. Record databases, date ranges, search terms, and language restrictions. Note publication and language bias.

Examiners' top complaints: failure to use recent literature, parochialism, excluding landmark studies, no critical assessment.

LR-introduction template: topic statement, scope refinement, named themes, narrowed focus. Identify your own two or three organising themes.

LR-body template: Source A reports X; Source B finds otherwise; a methodological explanation for the contradiction; Source C adjudicates. Use this shape for any contested claim in your field.

### Methodology

Sources: Dissertation Structure and Argument, Aims and Research Questions, Reflexivity for Validity, Methodology and Trustworthiness, Programme Remit and Positioning, Research Ethics Scaffold.

The Methodology is usually the best place to start writing.

Justify your design over the alternatives your programme offers (for example, a qualitative design over a computational one). Defend the choice explicitly.

Quality criteria: use trustworthiness, credibility, dependability rather than validity and reliability. Triangulation supports dependability; reflexivity supports validity; transparency is the obligation, not a metric.

Reflexivity: stage by stage (planning, sample, interviewing, analysis, reporting), each stage getting its own accounting. Foreground reflexivity rather than relegating it to an appendix. Use Pillow's four strategies and Reinharz's three selves as structuring vocabulary.

Sample-validity move: disclaim generalisability; reframe the sample as yielding "rich and unique data" rather than representative data.

Reflexive journal entries: embedded inline in Methodology and Findings, not appendix-only. Per-incident, roughly 250 to 400 words, themed by reflexive function rather than dated. Move sequence per entry: describe the moment, name the emotion or bias, hook to a theoretical category, state what changed, name a residual limit.

RQ-method alignment: each RQ guides method choice. Build an RQs grid with columns for Research Question, Data Collection, Data Analysis and Interpretation, and Outcomes.

### Ethics (within Methodology or as a standalone section)

Sources: Research Ethics Scaffold, Submission Compliance and Data Protection, Programme Remit and Positioning.

State your risk level and justify it (for example, low-risk: non-clinical, non-vulnerable, non-deceptive, adult participants, public material).

Two-part architecture: design ethics (what was built into the study) and in-practice ethics (judgement calls during fieldwork).

Seven-issue scaffold: autonomy and informed consent; confidentiality and anonymity; power and vulnerability; incentives; conflict of interest; harms to the researcher; data management.

Confidentiality versus anonymity: quoting public material verbatim can be de-anonymising even with no name attached. Name this complication.

Vulnerability is contextual, not categorical: design for the situational and relational vulnerability participants may experience even when they are not "vulnerable" in the institutional sense.

Procedural sequence: supervisor consultation, then ethics approval, then consent.

Cite your discipline's professional ethics guidelines where applicable.

### Findings

Sources: Dissertation Structure and Argument, Reflexive Journal Writing.

Each substantive chapter has a clear opening and closing signalling three things: (a) how the chapter fits the dissertation, (b) the chapter's internal structure, (c) the main takeaway.

Treatment must be analytical, integrating conceptual, empirical, and methodological insight, either through the body or in a dedicated analysis chapter.

Embed reflexive journal entries inline at the moments where they bear on the analysis.

### Discussion

Sources: Argumentation and Critical Thinking, Dissertation Structure and Argument, Literature Review Craft.

The (a) to (f) test: has the chapter found its own position relative to the categorised evidence? Step (f) is where most Discussion chapters fail.

Counter-argument: "Whereas X suggests..., it seems to me that...". Use sources to define your position, not to pile up.

Argument levels: state explicitly which level your primary contribution sits at (often Conceptual plus Methodological).

Adjudicate between the contested accounts the LR set up. Do not just recap.

### Conclusion

Sources: Dissertation Structure and Argument, Aims and Research Questions.

Often best written last, alongside the Introduction.

Reflect on the implications of the findings for wider theory and, where relevant, for practice.

Pick up the themes about the study's rationale from the Introduction.

Scale outcomes to the RQs and keep them modest at MSc scale ("a description or account of...", "a framework for...", "guidelines for..."). Avoid claiming changes in policy or "proof that...".

## Cross-cutting craft

### Reading and note-taking
Triage every paper before deep reading (Source, Title, Abstract, Graphics, Express read). Separate factual notes from critical notes. Translate high-value sources into thesis-able prose while the material is fresh. (See [[Effective Reading Protocol]].)

### Argumentation
Single authorial voice. Critical thought in every chapter. Connections explicit, not implied. A visible "golden thread" via signposting (the dispositio): sub-headed signposting and recurring callbacks to the central claim across chapters. (See [[Argumentation and Critical Thinking]].)

### Quotations and referencing
Quotations sparingly; over 40 words becomes a block quote, indented and single-spaced. Page numbers required even when not quoting, unless referring to the gist. Bibliography alphabetical by first-author surname, every citation matched to an entry. (See [[Dissertation Structure and Argument]].)

### Prose mechanics
Treat "data" as plural. Distinguish "i.e." (closes a set) from "e.g." (opens a non-exhaustive set). "Try to", not "try and". "Fewer" countable, "less" non-countable. "Different from". No exclamation marks. "However" is an adverb: it needs a full stop or semicolon before it and cannot splice clauses with a comma. Singular "they" over "his or her". No full stop after headers. Prefer lean, active-voice prose; keep filler connectives and showy punctuation to a minimum. (See [[Academic Grammar and Punctuation]].)

## Verification checklist (run against your own project)

1. **Word count.** Confirm your dissertation's word count against your programme handbook, Learn pages, and supervisor. Do not assume the generic 15,000-word example applies.
2. **RQ count and shape.** Verify total count is 3 to 4, each is one question (no hidden conjunctions), none starts with "how effective" or "evaluate", and each maps to one key word of a single overall aim.
3. **Comparative or evaluative verb in the aim.** If your aim leads with "compare" or "evaluate", either re-verb it to "understand" or "identify", or defend the comparison's tractability explicitly in the Introduction.
4. **Annotated bibliography.** Confirm with your supervisor or programme whether one is required before building it.
5. **Submission, ethics, and data-protection specifics.** Verify the deadline, pass thresholds, ethics risk-level labels, and data-management rules against your own programme handbook.
