# Effective and Efficient Reading Protocol

*Reusable craft reference. Derived from a UK university study resource on effective and efficient reading for research. Topic-neutral. Feeds the literature-review pipeline (read, then note, then synthesise).*

## What it informs

All chapters. Most directly the Literature Review (reading and synthesis) and the Methodology (note-taking discipline). Reading-pile triage throughout.

## Structural rules and framing moves

- **Every reading task starts with a defined purpose.** Why did you choose to read this? What are you reading it FOR? Clarify before opening the document.
- **Triage every paper via STAGE before deep reading.** Source, Title, Abstract, Graphics, Express read.
- **Treat papers differentially by value to the thesis.** High-value papers get deep notes; peripheral ones get pass-overs. Calibrate effort to "so what" weight.
- **Separate factual notes from critical notes** at the note-taking stage (what the author says versus what you think of it).
- **Translate high-value sources into thesis-able prose while the material is fresh.** Two to three lines, up to one page, retrievable during writing without re-reading the source.
- **Use a standardised note proforma** capturing Catalogue, Purpose, Objective, Relevance, Evidence, Usage, and an Action Code (1 = detailed analysis, 2 = important general, 3 = minor, 4 = not relevant).
- **Apply a comprehension check after each section** (Value, Opinion, Translate-and-Explain).
- **Critical-processing demands.** Provenance, Objectivity, Persuasiveness, Value.
- **Distinguish predictable side-tracks from the core focal area.** "Follow-the-references" drift is the named failure mode.

## Exemplar passages worth modelling

> "All literature notes can be stored in a single file (or thematically grouped) and can then be retrieved and used in the academic writing phase of research. Engaging in this process while the source material is still fresh in the mind minimizes accidental rework months or years later."

Justifies a quote-anchored, retrievable note architecture: translate while fresh, retrieve at the writing phase.

> "At note-taking stage, can you separate out your 'factual' notes (i.e. what the author says) from your 'critical' ones (i.e. what you think of their work)?"

Maps onto a split between factual notes (quotes, claims) and critical notes (your synthesis). Keep the two visually separate.

> "How does the author structure the argument? Can you 'deconstruct' the flow of the argument? Has the author evaluated enough literature relevant to the problem/issue? Does the author include literature taking opposing positions?"

A critical-reading checklist for assessing each source's argument structure rather than just summarising it.

## Caveats

- The speed-reading section is largely irrelevant for dissertation craft. Dense qualitative or theoretical sources need slow reading, not scanning. Skip it.
- The framework is generic postgraduate, not discipline-specific. Nothing here distinguishes social-science qualitative reading from STEM paper triage.
- The "thesis-able prose" tip risks blurring the author's voice with your own. Keep verbatim quotes anchored to page numbers rather than relying on paraphrase alone, so you can always recover what the source actually said.
