# Rules

Binding rule book for drafting a taught-Masters (MSc/MA) dissertation. The craft below is distilled from UK MSc dissertation-writing guidance. The structural typology, the (a)-(f) argument test, and the prose-mechanics grammar rules draw on University of Edinburgh handbook material; the ethics scaffold draws on standard qualitative-methods sources. All of it is reusable across topics.

> [!info] How to use this pack
> This is topic-neutral machinery. Where a rule shows a bracketed placeholder like [your topic], [population], or [your case], fill it with your own. Where it cites a craft framework by name (Andrews, Gaw, Guillemin and Gillam), that reference is reusable as-is. Institution-specific items (word counts, handbook clause numbers, named ethics committees, approval levels, required citation style) are marked "verify against your own programme handbook."

## 1.1 Argument shape (cross-cutting)

Choose an overarching structure from the Edinburgh handbook's six-structure typology (verify the full list against your own handbook; named examples within the typology include **Comparative**, **Causal**, and **Evaluative**). A dissertation usually has one dominant structure with sub-sections that use others. A Comparative dissertation, for example, might use Causal reasoning in the analysis chapter and Evaluative reasoning in the Discussion.

State your argument level explicitly: **Conceptual**, **Methodological**, **Empirical**, or a combination. A conceptual claim reframes how a phenomenon is understood; a methodological move pairs or adapts methods. Name yours.

The **(a) to (f) test** (Andrews) is the diagnostic for whether a chapter is doing argumentative work or merely exposition. Every chapter must reach step **(f)**, finding the dissertation's own position relative to the categorised evidence. Stopping at step **(e)**, arraying evidence into categories, is exposition, not argument.

The "golden thread" must remain visible across chapters. Aggressive sub-headed signposting. Recurring callbacks to the central claim.

Single authorial voice. Critical thought in every chapter. Connections explicit, not implied.

## 1.2 Structure and word count

Generic structure (verify the programme-specific word count and required chapters against your handbook or supervisor).
- Introduction
- Substantive chapters (Literature Review, Methodology, Findings, Discussion)
- Conclusion
- References, appendices

Build a word-count allocation plan early, budgeting words per chapter against your programme's total. Verify the total: many UK taught-Masters dissertations sit in roughly the 10,000 to 15,000 word range, but this varies by programme.

Each substantive chapter has a clear opening and closing that signal three things.
1. How the chapter fits into the rest of the dissertation.
2. The chapter's internal structure.
3. The main takeaway.

Methodology is the best place to start the writing process. Introduction and Conclusion are best written last.

A word-count penalty is typically automatic (verify your programme's penalty rule). Stay under the specified limit.

## 1.3 Research question and aim

One overall aim. One sentence. Maximum 3 to 4 research questions.

Aim must be SMART. Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Tractable.

Aim verbs to choose from. `understand`, `investigate`, `describe`, `explore`, `identify the factors that`, `consider the benefits and challenges of`. Avoid `evaluate` and `compare` as primary verbs at MSc scale, even when the dissertation has comparative content. Lead with `understand` or `identify the factors that` instead.

Each RQ must.
- Be answerable
- Have a potentially interesting answer
- Be on the right scale (not too big, not too small, not yes-or-no, not too open or too closed)
- Be one question only (no implicit conjunctions)
- Guide method choice
- Have a clear relationship to the aim

RQ tests.
- Specific and clear, not vague
- Feasible with the proposed design
- Unanswered, the research enigma exists
- Important, the stakes are clear (socially, politically, theoretically)

RQ stems worth using. "How do [population] in [your setting] ...?", "What [features/factors] of [your case] shape ...?", "To what extent ...?". Avoid "How effective ...?".

Build an RQ grid early. Columns for Research Question, Data Collection, Data Analysis and Interpretation, Outcomes. One source of data can answer more than one RQ. One RQ may need more than one source.

## 1.4 Literature review

Three-part structure.
1. Introduction states scope and what is excluded. No source citations needed in the intro.
2. Main body synthesises across sources.
3. Conclusion adjudicates, identifies gaps, or segues into own research. Does not recap.

Synthesis, not laundry list. Replace serial paraphrase with integrated discussion. Use signpost connectors ("Similarly", "In contrast", "Concurred with"). If your style discipline bars connectors like `however`, `furthermore`, `moreover` (see §1.9), use new sentences instead.

Every paragraph opens with a topic sentence stating what the paragraph will do.

Two moves required to demonstrate critical engagement.
1. Integration via similarities and differences across sources.
2. Analytical step-back asking "so what does that all mean?", "why are there conflicting findings?", "what is missing?".

Source-selection logic must be documented even for a narrative review. Inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, databases, date ranges, search terms, language restrictions. Note publication and language bias as caveats on coverage.

Examiners' top complaints. Failure to use recent literature. Parochialism. Excluding landmark studies. No critical assessment.

LR-intro template (Gaw). Topic statement. Scope refinement. Named themes. Narrowed focus. Name your dissertation's core themes (its pillars) here.

LR-middle template (Gaw). Source A reports X. Source B finds otherwise. Methodological explanation for the contradiction. Source C adjudicates. Direct template for engaging contested claims.

Counter-positioning move (Andrews). "Whereas X suggests ..., it seems to me that ...". Sources are used to define your position, not to pile up.

## 1.5 Methodology

Justify your chosen design over the alternatives you rejected. If you choose a qualitative design over a computational or quantitative one (or the reverse), defend the choice explicitly against the credible alternatives for your question.

Quality criteria use the vocabulary appropriate to your paradigm. For qualitative work, use **trustworthiness, credibility, dependability** instead of validity and reliability. Triangulation supports dependability. Reflexivity supports validity. Transparency is the obligation.

Reflexivity is expected in qualitative work and is often mandated by ethics guidance (verify your programme's requirement). Stage by stage. Planning, sample, interviewing or fieldwork, analysis, reporting. Each stage gets its own reflexive accounting. Foreground reflexivity rather than relegating it to an appendix.

Reflexive journal entries embedded inline at the moments where they bear on the analysis. Per-incident, around 250 to 400 words, themed by reflexive function rather than dated. Move sequence per entry. Describe the moment. Name the emotion or bias. Hook to a theoretical category. State what changed. Name a residual limit.

Sample-validity move. Disclaim generalisability. Reframe the sample as yielding rich and unique data rather than representative data.

Each RQ guides method choice via the RQ grid.

## 1.6 Ethics

Frame your study's ethics-risk level and justify why (approval levels and committee names are institution-specific, verify against your own ethics committee). A low-risk justification typically rests on: non-clinical, non-vulnerable, non-deceptive, adult participants, and public posts or public data.

Two-part architecture (Guillemin and Gillam 2004).
1. **Design ethics**, what was built into the study.
2. **In-practice ethics**, judgement calls during fieldwork.

Seven-issue scaffold. Address each, even briefly.
1. Autonomy and informed consent
2. Confidentiality and anonymity
3. Power and vulnerability
4. Incentives
5. Conflict of interest
6. Harms to researcher
7. Data management

Confidentiality is not anonymity. Quoting public posts verbatim is potentially de-anonymising even if no name is attached. Name this complication where it applies to your data.

Vulnerability is contextual, not categorical. Participants who are not obviously "vulnerable" can become situationally vulnerable through the topic itself (for example employer surveillance, community reprisal, or exposure and doxxing risk for those discussing sensitive or contentious matters). Treat as situational and relational.

Procedural sequence. Supervisor consultation, then ethics approval, then consent.

Cite your discipline's professional ethics guidance where applicable (for UK social research, for example the BSA Statement of Ethical Practice and ESRC ethics guidance).

## 1.7 Findings, discussion, conclusion

Findings chapters integrate conceptual, empirical, and methodological insights. Embed reflexive journal entries inline.

Discussion must reach Andrews step (f). Find the dissertation's own position relative to the categorised evidence. Adjudicate between contested accounts. Do not just recap.

Conclusion reflects on implications for wider theory and where relevant for practice. Picks up themes from the Introduction. Outcomes scaled to RQs. Modest at MSc scale. Avoid claims of policy change or "proof that".

## 1.8 Quotations and referencing

Quotations sparingly. Only when wording is striking. Over 40 words is a block quote, indented, single-spaced (verify the block-quote threshold and formatting against your required style guide).

Page numbers required in every citation, even when not directly quoting.

Bibliography in alphabetical order by first author surname. Every citation matched to an entry.

References use one academic format consistently (for example Harvard / Cite Them Right). Follow the style your programme requires.

## 1.9 Prose mechanics

The list below is an example style discipline, adjust to taste. It overlays a personal style preference onto a grammar and punctuation guide (here the Edinburgh G&P guide). Where a personal style preference and a grammar guide conflict, decide which governs and apply it consistently.

**Voice.** Active voice only. First-person `I` permitted in methodology and reflexivity sections, sparingly elsewhere. Single authorial voice throughout.

**Reduce colons, semicolons, and em-dashes.** The triplet is a strong AI-prose tell. Prefer full sentences and parentheses. Hyphens for compound modifiers stay.

**Example banned words and phrases** (an example discipline, adjust to taste). `can`, `may`, `just`, `that`, `very`, `really`, `entirely`, `actually`, `certainly`, `probably`, `basically`, `could`, `maybe`, `derive`, `embark`, `enlightening`, `esteemed`, `shed light`, `craft`, `imagine`, `realm`, `game-changer`, `unlock`, `discover`, `skyrocket`, `not alone`, `revolutionize`, `disruptive`, `utilize`, `dive deep`, `tapestry`, `illuminate`, `unveil`, `pivotal`, `intricate`, `elucidate`, `hence`, `furthermore`, `however`, `harness`, `exciting`, `groundbreaking`, `cutting-edge`, `remarkable`, `glimpse into`, `navigating`, `landscape`, `stark`, `testament`, `in summary`, `in conclusion`, `moreover`, `boost`, `opened up`, `powerful`, `inquiries`, `ever-evolving`.

**Academic-prose rules** (Edinburgh G&P guide).
- Treat `data` as plural. "The data are X", not "the data is X".
- `i.e.` closes a set. `e.g.` opens a non-exhaustive set. They are not interchangeable.
- `try to`, not `try and`.
- `fewer` for countable, `less` for non-countable.
- `different from`, not `different to` or `different than`.
- No exclamation marks.
- `however` is an adverb, not a conjunction. It cannot splice clauses with a comma.
- Singular `they` is preferred over `his or her`.
- No full stop after headers.
- Comma splices and run-ons avoided.
- Parallelism in lists and bullets. Every item shares part-of-speech and tense.

**Length and rhythm.** Short sentences. Lean prose. Concrete examples over abstractions. No padding adjectives or adverbs. No setup phrases. No trailing summaries.

## 1.10 Compliance checklist for any dissertation chapter draft

Before declaring any chapter draft complete, check.

- [ ] Chapter opens with a clear statement of (a) fit in dissertation, (b) chapter structure, (c) main takeaway.
- [ ] Chapter closes with a tie-up that links to the next chapter.
- [ ] Argument structure is identifiable.
- [ ] Topic-sentence paragraphs throughout.
- [ ] LR-style synthesis, not laundry-list paraphrase.
- [ ] Counter-positioning moves where the chapter engages contested claims.
- [ ] Page numbers in every in-text citation.
- [ ] Quotations sparingly, only when wording is striking.
- [ ] Bibliography entries match every in-text citation.
- [ ] Active voice. Lean prose. No banned words (per your chosen style discipline).
- [ ] No em-dashes. Colons and semicolons reduced. Hyphens fine.
- [ ] No exclamation marks. No `data is`. No `try and`.
- [ ] Reflexive accounting present where the chapter touches positionality.
- [ ] Ethics references where the chapter touches data and participants.
- [ ] Within the chapter's word allocation per your word-count plan.
